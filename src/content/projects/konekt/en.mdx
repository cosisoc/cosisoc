<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="People are rarely lonely for lack of opportunities, but because the first step is hard." wide statement>

In the course Invention 1 with Prof. Michael Schuster, the task was to find a problem ourselves and develop a solution for it. We worked across disciplines: three students of interaction design and one of product design.

The core idea was mine. People move far more often today than they used to. Whoever moves has to build a new circle of friends, and whoever stays loses family and friends who move away. At the same time, everyday comfort makes us less dependent on each other. Many natural occasions for contact disappear.

Our approach: new technology should also tackle problems that technology has helped create. Instead of yet another app that follows you everywhere, we wanted to develop a physical product for the home. You use it when you feel like it, and it brings people together in real life again.

<Chapter title="Research" teaser="Existing products replace contacts instead of enabling new ones." wide>

First we analysed what already exists for older people: KOMP, Familink, ElliQ and GrandPad. All of them simplify technology, but none addresses the actual problem. They rely on existing family contacts or simulate company, like the robot ElliQ.

This led to our guiding question: why do retirees stop taking part in social life, and how can taking part be made as easy as possible?

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Target group & personas" teaser="The problem is less the feeling than the loss of opportunities." wide>

Our target group is people between 65 and 95. In 2024 that was around 19 million people in Germany, and about one in three of them lives alone. We were especially interested in the transition into retirement. Several anchors fall away at once, such as work, a partner or friends, while a lot of free time suddenly appears.

We excluded people with advanced dementia or severe visual impairments. People whose fine motor skills no longer allow them to use a touchscreen reliably, for example because of a tremor, are explicitly included. That strongly shaped the interaction.

<Chapter title="Concept" teaser="Konekt invites instead of demanding planning." wide>

From the personas we derived three requirements. First, lower the barrier: the initiative needed should be as small as possible, so the device suggests meetups instead of making people search. Second, real connection: Konekt should not be used for as long as possible, but bring people back among others quickly. Third, suggestions based on interests, so they motivate rather than frustrate.

The suggestions come from a transparent weighting instead of machine learning: interests count for up to 40 points, mood up to 30, accessibility up to 20 and cost up to 10. From 75 points, a meetup is highlighted. Konekt also shows which acquaintances have already said yes, because it is easier to go somewhere when you know who will be there.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Interaction principle" teaser="Three gestures with a stone replace the touchscreen." wide>

Place: the stone is placed on the rail around the display and clicks into one of eight magnetic points you can feel. The element in front of it is highlighted, like a hover.

Slide: moving along the edge scrolls through lists, messages or days.

Press: pressing the stone confirms the selection, like a click. Originally, simply leaving the stone on a point was meant to confirm. That did not work well in tests with fellow students, so we switched to pressing.

<Chapter title="First functional prototype" teaser="The hardware should not dictate the interaction." image={ansatz1} label="Build of the first functional prototype: display, wooden board, acrylic and rail">

To test the interaction principle early, we first built with what was available: a rectangular touch display. A wooden board with a round cut-out simulated the round display. We screwed metal screws into the edge that worked as capacitive contact points and operated the display as soon as you touched them. It was a stopgap, but it let us try the edge interaction right away.

With the round display we had to rethink. A 3D-printed housing widened the edge, and on it we built a large voltage divider: depending on the position of the conductive stone, a different voltage was meant to be measured. Despite a lot of tuning of circuit and software, the analogue range stayed too unstable to reliably tell where the stone was.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Iteration and solution" teaser="From analogue to digital: 8 reed sensors instead of a voltage divider." wide>

Instead of measuring a value continuously, the third approach detects fixed positions. Eight reed sensors in the edge of the housing react to a magnet in the stone and report clearly via an Arduino where the stone is. This was stable and precise and covered two of the three gestures: placing and sliding.

For pressing, we built an ESP32-C6 with a button and battery into the 3D-printed stone. It sends the confirmation wirelessly over Wi-Fi to the system. Position detection and confirmation stay cleanly separated, and the click happens exactly where the stone is.

<Chapter title="Web app & system architecture" teaser="A web app that brings all inputs together.">

The interface runs as a web app in the browser on the Raspberry Pi. A central input manager unifies all signals: the position from the reed sensors, the click from the stone and, for testing, keyboard input. This let us develop the app before the hardware was ready.

In the prototype, events, profiles, weather and messages exist as test data. The events are recalculated every day from 22 templates, so the prototype looks current in every presentation. In the finished product, Konekt would collect events from online sources, newspapers and notice boards and filter them locally by location.

As soon as the app was running, fellow students tried out the prototype. Fast and careless use in particular revealed bugs that we were able to fix. I programmed with Claude Code in VS Code. AI-assisted programming delivers results quickly, but it requires a structured approach.

<Chapter title="Form finding" teaser="A piece of home furnishing, not an assistive device." wide>

Konekt should not stigmatise, but fit calmly into the home and deliberately not look like a smartphone. The moodboard draws on a Zen garden: matte plastic, light textile, wood and a few metallic details. The round display was a deliberate decision, because only then can the stone be guided along a rail all the way around the screen. The rail recalls the ripples that appear when a stone falls into water.

For the stone we built and tested foam models, and an ellipse sat best in the hand.

<Chapter title="Screen design" teaser="A round display, few elements, large type." wide>

Little space and unfamiliar layout patterns made the round display the biggest design challenge. Over many iterations, the main question was what really matters and what can go. The centre shows only the essentials, and texts have to be short and concrete. The suggestions sit on a spiral that starts at the present. Sliding the stone moves you further into the future, and meetups that match the chosen mood are highlighted.

The interface picks up the shape of the display: soft shadows, muted greens and violets, and Poppins in deliberately large sizes. Recurring elements such as confirm, decline and the placement arrow make the interaction predictable.

<Chapter title="Result" teaser="Two days of exhibition where visitors used Konekt themselves." wide>

In the end we had a functional prototype, a design prototype, a stop-motion video, a presentation and documentation.

At the two-day semester exhibition, older visitors also used the functional prototype. Although it did not yet match the design prototype in form and usability, several said they would use Konekt and asked when it would be available. We also presented Konekt together with three other projects on older people at an event organised by the city, among others to staff from care facilities. This feedback was informal and does not replace structured tests, but it shows that the idea meets a real need.

Thought further, but not built: AI summaries of group chats, and a key ring pendant that lets two people add each other as friends when they meet.

<Chapter title="Reflection" teaser="Better one part in depth than everything on the surface." wide>

Prototyping the whole concept at once was not possible, so we focused on the device, the stone and their interaction. Looking back, I would decide earlier what really needs to be built for the vision. I enjoyed going deep and refining details. That time could have made more of the concept visible elsewhere. The most important next step would be structured tests with people who are just retiring. The principle reaches beyond retirement, though: many younger people also find their phone use stressful.

</Chapter>
