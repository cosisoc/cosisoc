<Part title="Starting Point" />

<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="People are rarely lonely for lack of opportunities, but because the first step is hard." wide statement>

In the course Invention 1 with Prof. Michael Schuster, the task was to find a problem ourselves and develop a solution for it. We worked across disciplines: three students of interaction design and one of product design.

People move far more often today than they used to. Whoever moves has to build a new circle of friends, and whoever stays loses family and friends who move away. At the same time, everyday comfort makes us less dependent on each other.

New technology should also tackle problems that technology has helped create. Instead of yet another app that follows you everywhere, we wanted to develop a physical product for the home.

<Chapter title="Research" teaser="Existing products replace contacts instead of enabling new ones." wide>

First we analysed what already exists for older people: KOMP, Familink, ElliQ and GrandPad. All of them simplify technology, but none addresses the actual problem. They rely on existing family contacts or simulate company, like the robot ElliQ.

This led to our guiding question: what barriers keep retirees from taking part in social life, and how can taking part be made as easy as possible?

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Target Group & Personas" teaser="Entering retirement, a big change in social life." wide>

Loneliness affects many stages of life. So we looked at several groups, including teenagers. We were most interested in the start of retirement, because hardly any other transition changes social life as much. Several anchors fall away at once: work and with it colleagues, routine and everyday life. In return, there is suddenly a lot of free time waiting to be filled.

So our target group is people between 65 and 95. In 2024 that was around 19 million people in Germany, and about one in three of them lives alone. We excluded people with advanced dementia or severe visual impairments. People with unsteady fine motor skills, for example because of a tremor, are explicitly included. That strongly shaped the interaction.

<Part title="Concept" />

<Chapter title="Concept" teaser="Konekt: access to the community" wide>

Konekt is a central point of contact that anyone can use. It shows older people what is happening around them and makes it easier to take the step to get there.

1. **Visibility:** All events nearby, gathered in one place.
2. **Inhibition:** If you know beforehand who will be there, you are more likely to go.
3. **Accessibility:** A community only grows if nobody is left behind. So the medium has to be usable by everyone, with or without a smartphone.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Interaction Principle" teaser="Three gestures with a stone replace the touchscreen." wide>

Our goal was to bring digital content into the tangible world: the device should be operated by placing something. Ideation rounds such as Crazy 8s produced many, sometimes very wild ideas. An early idea with several bowls quickly became too complicated. What remained was the simplest: a single stone that you put down and guide along the edge of a round display. The circular movement is intuitive, and just three gestures control everything.

**Place:** The stone clicks into one of eight magnetic points on the edge that you can feel. The element in front of it is highlighted, like a hover.<br />
**Slide:** Moving along the edge scrolls through lists, messages or days.<br />
**Press:** Pressing the stone confirms the selection, like a click. Originally, simply leaving the stone in place was meant to confirm. Tests showed, however, that a fixed waiting time doesn't suit everyone, because some people need longer to read, and that it doesn't feel like a real decision. A deliberate gesture was needed.

<Chapter title="Iteration and Solution" teaser="From analogue to digital: 8 reed sensors instead of a voltage divider." wide>

Our first approach worked like a giant potentiometer: a rail around the display was built as a large voltage divider. Depending on the stone's position, a different voltage resulted, from which we calculated the position. This basically worked, but the values fluctuated too much to rely on.

So we switched to fixed positions. Eight reed sensors in the edge of the housing react to a magnet in the stone and report clearly via an Arduino where the stone is. This was stable and precise and covered two of the three gestures: placing and sliding.

For pressing, we built an ESP32-C6 with a button and battery into the 3D-printed stone. It sends the confirmation wirelessly over Wi-Fi to the system. Position detection and confirmation stay cleanly separated, and the click happens exactly where the stone is.

<Chapter title="Web App & System Architecture" teaser="A web app that brings all inputs together.">

The interface runs as a web app in the browser on the Raspberry Pi. All inputs come together in one place: the position from the reed sensors, the click from the stone and, for testing, the keyboard.

In the prototype, events, profiles, weather and messages are test data. The events are regenerated every day from 22 templates, so the prototype looks current in every presentation. In the finished product, Konekt would collect events from online sources, newspapers and notice boards and filter them by location.

As soon as the app was running, fellow students tried out the prototype. Especially those who used it quickly and carelessly uncovered bugs that we were then able to fix. I programmed with Claude Code in VS Code. That delivered results quickly, but only worked with a well-thought-out system architecture.

<Chapter title="Kaius" teaser="How can we take away the fear of interaction?" image={kaiusFigur} fit="contain" label="Kaius, a small figure with eyes and a mouth">

In the concept, the device can also be operated by voice. But older people in particular often find it hard to talk to a device, and it isn't clear when it is even listening. That's why we developed Kaius, a small figure with eyes and a mouth. When Kaius is visible, the device is listening and waiting for input. When he isn't, it isn't listening either.

At the same time, Kaius takes away the inhibition of speaking into a device. You aren't talking to a machine, but to someone who listens. The human features are deliberately only hinted at.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Form Finding" teaser="A piece of home furnishing, not an assistive device." wide>

Konekt should not stigmatise, but fit calmly into the home and deliberately not look like a smartphone. The moodboard draws on a Zen garden: matte plastic, light textile, wood and a few metallic details. The round display was a deliberate decision, because only then can the stone be guided along a rail all the way around the screen. The rail recalls the ripples that appear when a stone falls into water.

For the stone we built and tested foam models, and an ellipse sat best in the hand.

<Chapter title="Screen Design" teaser="A round display, few elements, large type." wide>

Little space and unfamiliar layout patterns made the round display the biggest design challenge. Over many iterations, the main question was what really matters and what can go. The centre shows only the essentials, and texts have to be short and concrete. The suggestions sit on a spiral that starts at the present. Sliding the stone moves you further into the future, and meetups that match the chosen mood are highlighted.

The interface picks up the shape of the display: soft shadows, muted greens and violets. Recurring elements such as confirm, decline and the placement arrow make the interaction predictable.

The screen design was mainly done by Paula. I helped design it and implemented the screens in code.

<Part title="Result" />

<Chapter title="Result" teaser="Two days of exhibition where visitors used Konekt themselves." wide>

In the end we had a functional prototype, a design prototype, a stop-motion video, a presentation and documentation.

At the two-day semester exhibition, older visitors also used the functional prototype. Although it did not yet match the design prototype in form and usability, we received a lot of positive feedback on our concept. We also presented Konekt together with three other projects on older people at an event organised by the city, among others to staff from care facilities. This feedback was informal and does not replace structured tests, but it shows that the idea meets a real need.

Thought further, but not built: AI summaries of group chats, and a key ring pendant that lets two people add each other as friends when they meet.

<Chapter title="Reflection" teaser="Better one part in depth than everything on the surface." wide>

Prototyping the whole concept at once was not possible, so we focused on the device, the stone and their interaction. Looking back, I would decide earlier what really needs to be built for the vision. I enjoyed going deep and refining details. That time could have made more of the concept visible elsewhere. The most important next step would be structured tests with people who are just retiring. The principle reaches beyond retirement, though: many younger people also find their phone use stressful.

</Chapter>
