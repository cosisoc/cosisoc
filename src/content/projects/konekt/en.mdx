<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="Loneliness is rising – not for lack of opportunities, but because the first step is hard. Konekt lowers exactly that barrier.">

Retirement often removes several anchors at once: the working day, routines, sometimes a partner or friends. Konekt suggests suitable meetups nearby, shows which acquaintances have already said yes, and makes organising your own meetup a matter of a few moves.

The goal is not to keep people at the device, but to get them back among people as quickly as possible. Created in the course Invention 1 with Prof. Michael Schuster, together with product design.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Research" teaser="Instead of interviewing retirees, we questioned AI personas – and derived three requirements.">

A market analysis (KOMP, Familink, ElliQ, GrandPad) showed that no product tackles loneliness directly – they replace missing contacts with family or technology. We deliberately skipped real interviews: the topic is sensitive and access was difficult. Instead, we built four detailed personas, including a relative, and questioned them as AI characters in simulated interviews.

Two insights shaped the concept: people are reluctant to go somewhere when they don't know who will be there. And information is scattered – in the newspaper, the local bulletin, the club calendar. This led to three requirements: **lower the threshold, real connection instead of attachment to the device, suggestions based on interests.**

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Interaction Concept" teaser="Three gestures with a stone replace the touchscreen: place, slide, press.">

Placing highlights an element – like a hover. Sliding scrolls, along a line or in a circle. Pressing confirms. A magnetic track around the display guides the stone across 8 contact points that change meaning from screen to screen. Picking a day works as a spiral: each circular movement moves one day ahead.

The character Kaius shows when the device is listening – and without its face, it isn't. Tests with fellow students improved the interaction once more.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Interface" teaser="A round display, few elements, large type – with soft shadows that echo the physical interaction.">

The interface uses neumorphism, muted greens and violets, and Poppins at deliberately large sizes. Recurring elements – confirm, decline, placement arrow – make the interaction predictable.

From the sleep mode with a clock to the greeting with the weather to the event overview ("Manfred and Birgit are joining too"), every screen shows only what's needed. The screens were designed by the team; my part was turning them into code.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Prototyping" teaser="Three attempts to a stable solution. Our principle: the hardware must not dictate the interaction.">

First a rectangular display under a wooden board with a round cut-out, screws in the rim as contact points. Then a round display with a 3D-printed rim and a large voltage divider – the analogue reading was too unstable. The solution: 8 reed sensors and a magnet in the stone, stable and precise.

In parallel, the design prototype took shape: a wooden station (320 × 230 mm) whose track recalls the ripples of a stone dropped in water, and an elliptical stone found through sketches and foam models.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Technical Implementation" teaser="Sensors detect the stone, a web app shows what happens – cleanly separated and coded with Claude Code in VS Code.">

I built the app, the sensor integration, the Raspberry Pi with its own Wi-Fi and the wireless link to the stone. An Arduino reads 8 reed sensors and reports the stone's position. An ESP32 inside the stone detects the press and sends it via the Raspberry Pi's hotspot. A central input manager merges all signals, including keyboard input for testing.

The app runs in the browser. Events are generated for each day from 22 templates, so the prototype always feels current. An interface for AI is prepared.

Claude Code was the only way this scope was possible in one semester. AI-assisted coding delivers real results fast, but demands a lot of thinking along: without a structured approach it's easy to lose track.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Result" teaser="Two days of exhibition where visitors used Konekt themselves – including people from the target group who said they'd buy it.">

The outcome: a functional prototype, a design prototype, a presentation and a documentation. We deliberately focused on device, stone and interaction and worked them out in depth instead of touching on everything.

*Thought further, but not built:* voice input modelled on an answering machine, texts read aloud, an AI that summarises group chats and picks up appointments (with a local AI as fallback), and a key-ring tag – hold two together at a meetup and you're connected.

The principle reaches beyond retirement: many younger people find their phone use draining, too. A device that only lets through what really matters could help them as well.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="My Role" teaser="Research, concept, interaction principle and the entire technical implementation.">

I co-built the research and the AI personas, co-developed the interaction principle and implemented all of the technology: app, sensors, Raspberry Pi and system architecture.

Above all, I learned how to approach concept work in a structured way and keep a large team project organised. Next time I would decide earlier what really needs to be built for the vision: I enjoyed going deep and refining details – time that could have made more of the concept visible elsewhere. After all, a prototype should above all show what you set out to do.

</Chapter>
