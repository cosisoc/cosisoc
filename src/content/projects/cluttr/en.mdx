<Part title="Starting Point" />

<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="An unloved chore should become a game." wide statement>

In the course Interface Design 1 in our second semester, our task was to develop a controller for a given robot. The robot was fixed: mecanum wheels that let it drive in every direction (including sideways), and a gripper arm that opens, closes and adjusts in height. We could choose the use case and target group ourselves. The focus was a controller that makes all of the robot's functions intuitive to use.

<Chapter title="Target Group & Concept" teaser="Children aged 8 to 12." wide>

We found children especially interesting as a target group, precisely because a robot fits the theme so well. Our idea was a controller that invites play, of course, but through the fun also encourages tidying up.

</Chapter>

<Part title="Concept" />

<Chapter title="Finding the Form" teaser="First sketches of the shape and layout of the controls." wide>

Children already know controllers from games. So we based the controls on familiar gamepads instead of inventing our own logic.

In our first design, steering the robot through space played a bigger role than controlling the gripper arm.

<Chapter title="First Prototype & User Tests 1" teaser="First test whether the controls work, then design." image={prototype} label="Functional prototype made of wood" tint>

The first prototype, made of laser-cut wood, steers the real robot wirelessly. It only had to answer one question: are all functions within easy reach of two hands? Does the mapping of the functions feel natural?

We ran our first user tests with students at the HfG. Key learnings: we had oriented the Joy-Con the wrong way. Moving the robot through space worked well, but extending and retracting the gripper arm with the dial on the back of the controller was barely noticed. The position of the crab walk gave the function too much weight, given that it wasn't used very often and couldn't be used at the same time as the joystick.

Our idea of operating the gripper by closing your hand worked well. However, we reversed the direction so that closing your hand, i.e. pressing the limit switch, opened the gripper, and releasing it closed it again automatically. Some participants also struggled with the robot's speed on tasks that needed precision.

</Chapter>

<Part title="Iteration" />

<Chapter title="Iteration and Solution" teaser="Drive on the left, grab on the right." wide>

Our core idea was to split the robot's functions into two areas: steering the robot and controlling the gripper arm.

For simple controls, we decided to divide the functions between the controller's two grips. The left hand steers the robot, the right hand the gripper arm, clearly separated by colour. This made the dial, which had simply been overlooked in the previous version, more prominent. We also added a slow mode.

<Chapter title="User Tests 2" teaser="Second user tests: first students, then a fifth-grade class." image={usertest} label="User tests with a fifth-grade class">

With students we had checked the ergonomics. After that we tested with children, our actual target group.

The task: pick up a bottle and put it down inside a circle.

What children already know, they understand immediately. Children with controller experience solved the task much faster. That confirmed our decision to use familiar patterns.

Only the crab walk was new and wasn't understood. It got a label, and the speed button got an icon. All controls moved to the front.

While testing the gripper arm controls, we also realised how important it is to highlight controls with lighting and colour in the final product. That wasn't possible with our technical prototype.

</Chapter>
