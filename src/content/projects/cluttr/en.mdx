<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="An unloved chore should become a game." wide>

The robot from the Interface Design course was set: mecanum wheels that let it drive in every direction – including sideways – and a gripper that opens, closes and moves up and down. Use case and target group were up to us.

We chose children aged 8 to 11 who use the robot to tidy up their room. The focus was deliberately on the controller itself, not on an app or game mechanics around it.

<Chapter title="Concept" teaser="Drive on the left, grab on the right – splitting the functions shapes both form and colour." wide>

We started from controllers children already know – Nintendo Switch Joy-Cons, the toy Bop It – and lots of shape sketches. This led to a clear split: the left hand steers the robot, the right hand works the gripper. The split also shows in the colours.

*Thought further, but not built:* a display in the middle showing the robot's camera feed.

<Chapter title="Prototyping" teaser="From a laser-cut wooden board to a clay model to a design prototype in Blender." wide tint>

The first functional prototype consisted of two laser-cut wooden plates with spacers, its shape fitted to the components beforehand. In parallel, a clay model let us test how the controller sits in the hand.

The functional prototype controls the real robot wirelessly. Inside are a joystick, two crab-walk buttons in a 3D-printed housing, a slow-mode switch, a limit switch for the grasping motion and a potentiometer with a 3D-printed thumb wheel – connected via an Arduino and a shared power hub that saves cables.

After testing, all controls moved to the front. The final design was modelled in Blender – including several colour variants.

<Chapter title="User Testing" teaser="Two rounds of testing: first with students, then with an entire fifth-grade class.">

Testing with HfG students showed that the tilted joystick and the thumb wheel on the back didn't work – while the ergonomic shape and the split of functions did.

We then tested with a fifth-grade class, people with no connection to design. That gives different results from interviews within our own circle at the HfG. The task: steer the robot, pick up a bottle and put it down inside a circle.

Children with controller experience, for example from a Nintendo Switch, solved the task noticeably faster. So our controller follows conventions they already knew.

The tests led to two changes: the crab-walk button wasn't understood as expected and got a label. The speed button also got an icon.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Interaction" teaser="Every movement of the robot has its own tangible control." wide>

Left: the joystick drives, a button moves the robot sideways (crab walk), and a switch turns on slow mode for precise work.

Right: squeezing the grip closes the gripper – the hand's natural grasping motion carries straight over. A thumb wheel adjusts the height.

We rendered all controls in Blender. The back has a line texture.

<Chapter title="My Role" teaser="Design, sketching, prototyping, user tests and the 3D model in Blender." wide>

> “The children reacted differently from students – more directly, more playfully.”

I co-designed and sketched the form, built the prototypes with the team, co-ran the user tests and modelled the design prototype in Blender.

My biggest takeaway: how much testing with the real target group adds. The children also gave us new design ideas at the same time.
