<Chapter title="Overview" teaser="Reliable data, hidden behind poor usability." wide statement>

In the Application Design 1 course we rethought the WarnWetter app of the German Weather Service (DWD) – as a student project, not commissioned by the DWD. The app delivers reliable, official data, but it gets lost in the design: features like the pollen forecast are buried deep, overlays cover exactly what you want to see, and on the go – wearing gloves or with the dog on a leash – small graphics and the radar are hard to use.

</Chapter>

<Chapter title="Research & Needs" teaser="What people need is the here and now." wide>

To avoid designing past our users, we ran interviews and surveys and analysed app store reviews. Weather apps are mainly used for the here and now and the next day. For our persona Anna, a student who spends a lot of time outdoors in the Allgäu, the weather decides about safety in the mountains and her health. In a Value Proposition Canvas we set her biggest pain points – cluttered information and hard-to-reach details – against our solutions: personalisation, precise data visualisation and smart widgets.

<Chapter title="Concept" teaser="Understanding the weather, not just reading it." wide statement tint>

Until now, the app’s unofficial guiding principle was: protecting lives and property through reliable, official weather data. That still matters, but it is a very technical way of thinking. We made the concept more human: complex data turns into personal recommendations. The app goes from data supplier to personal companion that shows at a glance what you need today.

<Chapter title="Templates, Widgets & Locations" teaser="A template to start with, a place with a memory." wide>

When you first open the app, you pick a template such as hiking, everyday life or gardening – sensibly preconfigured and ready to use. If you want more, you adjust the widgets: the home screen shows a reduced view, a long press opens details and settings.

The most stubborn question was how locations and templates relate to each other. Our solution: all locations apply to all templates, but each template has its own favourites. When Anna opens the hiking template, the Nebelhorn is right at the top.

<Chapter title="Navigation & Information Architecture" teaser="Everything important stays one step away." wide>

For the navigation, it was important to us to deliberately separate locations and templates so that both are equally easy to reach. Our first sketches led to a flat structure: onboarding leads straight to the home screen with the active location. From there you reach locations and templates, the community profile and the settings.

<Chapter title="Design Principles" teaser="Four principles as the yardstick for every decision." wide tint>

From our pain points we collected adjectives and condensed them into four design principles. Then we deliberately opened up the design space, among other things with Claude Design. In the wealth of variants we lost clarity. So we checked every element against the principles again and sharpened the principles themselves.

<Chapter title="Colour as Language" teaser="Colour shows temperature and warning level at once." wide>

Throughout the system, colour is the most important means of communication. One scale encodes two levels: for temperature, blue stands for below 0 °C, green for 0 to 10 °C, yellow for 10 to 20 °C and red for above 20 °C. For warnings, the same scale shows the danger, from blue for none to red for critical.

The illustrations follow the same grid: the background colour reveals the temperature, the illustration on top the weather. This way two levels of data can be read at a glance without overloading the home screen.
